# BACKSTORY — How "Power Card" Came to Be

*The record behind the game — first titled "Vote or Fade Away," renamed "Power Card" the same day it was built. Written October 25, 2026, and continued that evening.*

## The night it started

This game was not designed in a studio. It grew out of a single all-night research session on **October 24–25, 2026**, in which a father preparing to vote in the November 3, 2026 midterm elections worked through the US–Iran war, the tariff shock, and his own voting decision — and then decided his children, all in their early 20s and facing this economy as new voters, deserved the same homework in a form they would actually pick up.

The session began with an audit. A long exchange with **Gemini 4 Argon** (war status, war × tariffs economics, historical analogues, red-vs-blue) was checked claim by claim against live sources. Verdict: its escalation architecture was strong, but its figures floated — it quoted the 10-year Treasury at 4.46% in one answer and 5.12% in the next (the tape: a 5.31% close on October 5, the highest since 2002), put mortgages at 6.3–6.5% (actual: ~7.3–7.4%), and treated its own earlier output as settled fact. Its voting answer, pressed for a verdict, handed the decision back as a list of questions.

**Manus** took the same questions and produced a competent economics treatise — stagflation mechanics, landed-cost math, semiconductor exposure, historical parallels (1970s, 1930s, 2018–23). It corroborated the core analysis and genuinely added three things: a wage-price-spiral diagnostic dashboard, the fertilizer and helium second-order channels, and a per-candidate caveat to any party-line advice. But on the question that started the night — *was the public maneuvered into this war, and by whom?* — it was silent, treating the war and the tariffs as weather rather than as choices with authors. Pressed for a voting verdict, it too declined, offering criteria instead of a conclusion.

What follows is the one-page brief the session produced — verified against EIA, BLS, New York Fed, IAEA, IMF, and market data — and then gamified, unchanged in substance, into the eight battles of this game and its D&D-style companion module (Module VF-1).

# Midterm Voting Brief — The Iran War, the Tariffs, and the Record

*Prepared October 25, 2026, from the Kendo–Mike discussion of October 24–25; updated the same day after review of Manus's parallel analysis. Figures verified against EIA, BLS, New York Fed, IAEA, IMF, and market data.*

## Bottom line

**Vote blue in the 2026 midterms — as a check, not an endorsement.** The ballot cannot command the war (the blockade is an executive naval instrument and continues either way), but it can condition war funding, contest open-ended tariff authority, and force the war's evidentiary record into the open through subpoena and oversight. Stated pivot: if Tehran were visibly collapsing, the calculus would flip. It is not. The check logic is about congressional control, not party loyalty: a candidate of any color who votes for unconditional war funding or tariff escalation fails the test and hasn't earned the vote.

## How we got here

- Under the JCPOA, Iran's breakout timeline stood at ~12 months, with the IAEA certifying compliance. The US withdrew on **May 8, 2018**, over explicit warnings.
- Iran then rolled back in announced, reversible steps: breakout compressed to weeks, ~441 kg of uranium enriched to 60% accumulated, inspector access lost. The stockpile is now unlocated and uninspected.
- The war began **February 28, 2026**, justified by the claim Iran was "within two weeks of having a nuclear weapon." That claim fuses three assertions: capability (real — 60% material exists), imminence (unsupported — "two weeks" describes at most producing material for one device, not a deliverable weapon; weaponization adds ~1–2+ years), and intent to threaten the US imminently (weakest — contradicted by intelligence assessments). IAEA Director General Grossi, March 2, 2026: "we don't see a structured program to manufacture nuclear weapons." The same administration declared the program "completely and totally obliterated" in June 2025, then "two weeks away" in February 2026. Both cannot be true. Negotiators were reportedly making significant progress hours before the strikes.
- The burden of proof for starting a war sits on the government starting it. On the public record, it was not met.

## The warnings — documented, quantified, ignored

- **EIA (the government's own data):** Hormuz carried ~21 million barrels/day — over a quarter of seaborne oil and ~20% of global LNG — with negligible bypass capacity. Bab el-Mandeb flows had already fallen from 9.3 to 4.2 million barrels/day by 2025 under Houthi attack: the second chokepoint's failure was demonstrated, not hypothetical.
- **Market forecasts, June 2025 (eight months before the decision):** JPMorgan — Hormuz closure could take oil to $120–$130. Lipow Oil Associates — $100+ oil, gasoline up ~$1.25/gallon, ~$4.50 national average, ~$6 in California.
- **What actually happened:** US gas went from $2.98 (Feb 28) to $4.55 (May); six states crossed $5; California crossed $6. The BLS gasoline index posted its largest monthly increase since tracking began in 1967. War-risk insurance rose from ~0.05% to over 5% of vessel value — insurers, not just navies, close straits.
- **The tariff compounding was already measured:** New York Fed economists estimated tariffs had added **2.9 percentage points** to consumer goods inflation by February 2026 — before the first strike. CPI: 2.4% in February → 3.8% by April.
- **The real transmission channel is the cost of capital:** the 10-year Treasury closed at 5.31% on October 5 (highest since 2002), term premium ~1.07 points (highest since 2010), 30-year mortgages ~7.3–7.4%, Brent ~$113–114. America is a net energy exporter; the war reaches households through rates, not empty pumps.
- **Second-order channels arrive late and hit essentials:** roughly a third of global fertilizer shipments transit Hormuz (IMF), so food prices lag the energy shock by months; helium and sulfur-linked inputs expose semiconductors and medical imaging. Inventories and spare capacity are already drawn down, so a renewed disruption shocks harder than the first.

## The manufactured-crisis question

The facts cited in 2026 were largely real; what was erased was their **authorship**. Iraq (2003) was an invented capability. Iran (2026) is a self-created trajectory — the predictable product of dismantling the one instrument measurably holding the program in check — presented as an external emergency. A coordinated *hidden* agenda is unprovable and unnecessary: open ideology, factional wins (trade hawks, Iran hawks), and indifference to documented costs explain the entire record, and the provable charge is the stronger one. Historical anchor, stated precisely: Smoot-Hawley (1930) deepened and prolonged the **Great Depression** through retaliation and collapsed world trade — the standing lesson on sweeping unilateral tariffs in a crisis.

**The charge that holds: warned in writing, by its own agencies, with numbers — and proceeded anyway.**

## Watch before November 3

Strait transit volumes and war-risk premiums; Brent; the 10-year yield and term premium; any declassified release supporting the "two weeks" claim; Tehran's posture heading into the midterms.

**Price surge or wage-price spiral?** The distinction decides how bad this gets. A temporary surge stays narrow; a spiral shows up as: core and services inflation accelerating (not just fuel and food), inflation spreading across most CPI categories, wage growth persistently outrunning productivity (rising unit labor costs), firms pricing for expected future costs, and long-term (5–10 year) inflation expectations unanchoring. If expectations stay anchored and services stay calm, the shock is a price-level hit; if those five move together, it has become a process — and central banks will be forced to keep rates higher for longer.

## From brief to battle

- The brief's eight load-bearing topics became the game's **8 battles**: the Strait slams shut; who pays tariffs; the JCPOA "who built the corner" timeline; the landed-cost pincer; fertilizer and helium aftershocks; the rate freeze; the spiral watch list; and the finale — record over label, what the midterms actually control. (The game later grew to 12 battles — see "The second harvest" below — and then to 16, in the continuation at the end of this file.)
- The heroes — **Maya** (Voting Arrows), **Dre** (Axe of Freedom), **Priya** (Sword of Youth) — are early-20s composites of the generation inheriting this economy: gig work and gas prices, warehouse shifts, 200 unanswered job applications.
- The boss, **The Fade**, is built from the brief's villains in literal form: red tape, tariff price tags, oil barrels, and a chained ballot box. In the D&D module, its minions are the Tariff Wraith, the Spiral Specter, the Rate-Freeze Golem, and the Disinfo Imp.
- The module's core mechanic, **Informed Strike** (+1 to hit, +1 damage for stating a real fact before you swing), is the session's thesis made playable: information is the weapon; the vote is how you swing it.
- Every run of the game ends where the session ended: a four-step real-world plan, and vote.gov. **Election Day: Tuesday, November 3, 2026.**

## The second harvest — key points behind Battles 9–12 and the Death Blows

The session held more verified material than eight battles could carry. When the game expanded, these are the key points that were extracted from it — stated as they were established that night, from the record.

1. **The forecast record → Battle 9, "Receipts: The Forecasts."** During the June 2025 strikes — eight months before the 2026 decision — JPMorgan published that a Hormuz closure could take oil to $120–$130, and Lipow Oil Associates projected roughly $4.50 national-average gasoline, ~$6 in California. The 2026 outcome tracked the published range: gasoline $2.98 on February 28 to a $4.55 peak in May. *Key point: the consequences were forecast in writing, with numbers, in advance.*
2. **The transmission channel → Battle 10, "The Invisible Tax."** The United States is a net energy exporter; the shock reached households through finance, not fuel supply. The 10-year Treasury closed at 5.31% on October 5, 2026 (highest close since 2002), the term premium reached ~1.07 points (highest since 2010), and 30-year mortgages held ~7.3–7.4%. *Key point: in a modern economy, a distant blockade is paid at the mortgage closing table.*
3. **The anatomy of a claim → Battle 11, "Two Weeks Away?"** A figure can be true while the conclusion built on it is not. Iran's ~441 kg of uranium enriched to 60% is real material, but enrichment is step one of three: weaponization, miniaturization, and a deliverable warhead add an estimated 1–2+ years. The IAEA's Director General stated on March 2, 2026 that the agency saw no structured program to manufacture nuclear weapons. *Key point: capability, imminence, and intent are separate assertions, with separate evidence — weigh them separately.*
4. **Buffers are finite → Battle 12, "The Empty Cushion."** By fall 2026, global oil inventories stood roughly 509 million barrels below pre-war levels, spare production capacity was drawn down, and insurance markets had already repriced war risk from ~0.05% to over 5% of vessel value. *Key point: a system that has spent its cushion answers the next shock at full force.*

**The Death Blow questions** were designed as logic tests rather than recall questions, one per hero, and gated: each appears only when The Fade is already below 15 hit points — the finishing argument has to be earned. **Maya's "Archer's Eye" is a timeline test** — compare breakout time under the nuclear agreement (~12 months, inspectors present, compliance certified) with the trajectory after its abandonment (weeks, ~441 kg at 60%, inspectors out), and judge which produced the emergency. **Dre's "Axeman's Ledger" is an arithmetic test** — sum an open-ended war, open-ended tariffs, and a $1.5 trillion defense topline, price the borrowing at the observed term premium, and find what breaks first for working families. **Priya's "Healer's Verdict" is a burden-of-proof test** — a government asking its people to bear a war's costs must evidence capability *and* imminence *and* intent; the record, examined piece by piece, is where that test is passed or failed.

## The Fade, enhanced — the flipping-sides file

When The Fade was given a full character sheet of his own, his abilities were drawn from a documented pattern in American foreign policy: a partner armed and praised in one decade becomes the adversary of a later one — sometimes holding American-supplied weapons. The episodes below are matters of historical record.

- **Afghanistan: the 1980s → 2001–2021.** Under Operation Cyclone, the United States — with Saudi funding and Pakistan's ISI — supplied the Afghan mujahideen with money, training, and weapons, including Stinger anti-aircraft missiles, to fight the Soviet occupation. They were publicly celebrated as "freedom fighters." Networks and commanders formed in that war became al-Qaeda and the Taliban; after 2001, the United States fought a twenty-year war against them. The weapons and expertise outlived the alignment that delivered them.
- **Iraq: the 1980s → 1991 and 2003.** During the Iran–Iraq war, the United States tilted toward Saddam Hussein's Iraq with satellite intelligence, dual-use exports, and diplomatic support. In 1991, a US-led coalition fought Iraq over Kuwait; in 2003, the United States invaded and overthrew him. The partner of one decade was the enemy of the next two.
- **Iran: 1953 → 1979 → 2026.** The 1953 coup, backed by the US and UK, removed Prime Minister Mossadegh and consolidated the Shah's rule. The 1979 revolution that overthrew the Shah defined itself against that intervention — and the Islamic Republic it created is the adversary of the 2026 war. The longest arc in the file runs three generations.
- **The occupation drift.** In Afghanistan (2001) and Iraq (2003), missions announced for a single stated purpose became occupations of a decade or more, and the stated reasons rotated as the years passed — security, stability, training, conditions for withdrawal — long after the original casus belli had been answered or had collapsed. The war stays; the reason rotates.
- **The war economy.** War mobilization has repeatedly coincided with surging employment and industrial output: the Second World War cut US unemployment from 14.7% (1940) to 1.2% (1944) and created some 17 million civilian jobs, overwhelmingly on federal contracts. That is why the machine has a constituency in every era — contracts mean local jobs, and major programs distribute them widely (the F-35's supply chain spans 45+ states), so the spending defends itself politically. The gains are real, and so is the bill: at the WWII peak the boom came with rationing and price controls, the national debt rose roughly five-fold between 1941 and 1945, and output measures activity, not welfare — much of what was built was stockpiled or destroyed. Dwight Eisenhower, the general who won that war, used his 1961 farewell address to warn against the "military-industrial complex" and its "unwarranted influence." A jobs program that must be fed is a policy that defends itself. The financing habit compounds across every era in this file: the federal debt has now passed **$40 trillion**, and in the modern era it is never actually paid down — it is refinanced, indefinitely, at whatever rate the market demands, which is one reason the term premium sits at ~1.07 and mortgages at ~7.4%. Interest on the debt is now among the largest single federal outlays, exceeding the defense budget, and it purchases nothing. Households ultimately carry the servicing bill through three pressure valves: higher taxes and fees over time, inflation that quietly taxes wages and savings, and higher-for-longer interest rates. The one exception proves the mechanism: after WWII the debt was not repaid either — it was outgrown, and inflated down as a share of GDP across decades of growth. That exit requires the growth. The ratio is the context that makes the headline number legible: in 2001, before 9/11, gross federal debt stood at roughly **57% of GDP** and the government ran a surplus — the debt was shrinking relative to the economy. Two decades of decisions followed — the post-9/11 wars fought on borrowed money, tax cuts, the 2008 crisis, the pandemic, and now the Iran war — and the ratio crossed 100% in the early 2010s and stands past **120% of GDP** today. Passing the bill to future generations is not a new habit; it is the long-standing one. Today's young voters did not make those decisions — they inherited the ratio, and the only lever they hold over it is who they send to Congress.

How the file became game mechanics:

- **War Drum Volume** — the fear-first drumbeat that precedes each episode in the file. It scales the Hope a wrong answer costs.
- **Sound Bite Spin** — the selling language. "Freedom fighters" is the file's central artifact: a label applied to the same kind of fighter in one decade and withdrawn in the next. In game, Spin raises The Fade's Defense and sharpens his taunts.
- **Mission Creep** — the occupation drift, mechanized: a Fade who survives a battle regains hit points, because staying is what he does.
- **Blowback Arsenal** — the pattern itself: periodically he fires the band's own gifted weapons back at it, and the battle log names the history the shot came from.
- **The rotating excuse** on his sheet — threat → liberation → stability → "we're already there" — mirrors the documented rotation of stated rationales across the long occupations.

*The labels change with the decade. The weapons remain.*

## The rest of the day — the rename and everything after

*The record above closed at midday on October 25. The game kept growing that afternoon and evening. This continuation keeps the record whole.*

### Sixteen battles

Four more battles completed the arc, drawn from the flipping-sides file and the debt passage:

- **Battle 13, "The Inheritance (Debt, in Context)"** — the ratio over the headline number: roughly 57% of GDP with a surplus in 2001, past 120% today. Its correct answer ends: "That's the power card."
- **Battle 14, "The War Chest"** — the war economy's jobs pitch, answered by its own ledger: rationing, price controls, a debt that quintupled.
- **Battle 15, "Freedom Fighters"** — Operation Cyclone: the label applied to the same kind of fighter in one decade, withdrawn in the next.
- **Battle 16, "The Long Fuse"** — the finale: 1953 → the Shah → 1979 → 2026, the longest arc in the file.

The Fade breaks at 40 HP once the sixteenth battle is done — or falls outright, at any point, to a landed Death Blow.

### Dice, camps, and gambits

The heroes' character-sheet sliders became real combat statistics (modifier = (stat − 8) ÷ 2, class-capped so no hero can be everything). Attacks roll a true three-dimensional d20 against The Fade's Defense; a natural 20 doubles the damage, and even a rolled miss lands a glancing blow — a correct answer always hurts him, because the fact is the weapon. Each hero carries their own hit points now: his counterattacks wound the fighter in the arena, a hero at zero is DOWN, and if all three fall, The Fade wins. Between battles the band camps — healing hands, blessings and omens rolled on a d4, sharpening — and Canvass, which rallies a friend and charges the Power Card. His own sheet gained a Hard Skill Level: his sliders preset high, his Defense raised, and Fake News Gambits planted in odd-numbered battles — claims the player must rule TRUE or FAKE before answering, with the documented correction shown either way; fall for one and he heals on the spot. Later that day the hero sliders were put on fixed point-buy pools — Maya 73, Dre 61, Priya 93 — so a hero can be rebalanced but never simply maxed: every strength is bought with a weakness. Still later, the dice themselves learned to be handled: press and hold to turn a die under your finger, flick and release and it tumbles with your momentum for a long second before settling — always onto the face the game honestly rolled.

### The Power Card, mechanized

The thesis became a mechanic. A five-pip charge track — the band's shared Power Card — fills only through informed play: a correct answer, a gambit correctly ruled FAKE, a Canvass at camp; a landed Death Blow fills it outright. Playing it at full charge deals exactly 12 damage, zeroes his Spin defense for two battles, and strikes his current excuse through — later builds put the strikethrough on his card itself, where it stays until his next excuse rotates in. The endings remember whether it was played: "Power Card: PLAYED — and it counted," or, in a loss with a full unplayed card, "A power card unplayed changes nothing." The final element of the credits is the thesis card itself: *Play the game. Make your move count. Play your power card in hand — cast your vote.*

### The name

That afternoon the game was renamed for its own thesis. The title is now **POWER CARD**; *Vote or Fade Away* remains as its subtitle — the choice the game is about, kept in view. The repository and its addresses kept the original name, so every link already shared still works; a fresh home-screen install now labels the icon "Power Card."

### Campaigns of Friends

The character sheets came alive. Above each hero's send-into-battle button sit three small animated scenes — one per move — and tapping one opens its campaign tale in a scrolling panel: nine medieval backstories in all, one per move, each carrying the full arc — the band together (Leo the Bard or Sam the Rogue riding along in at least one tale per hero), a place, a creature, the finishing move or the spell that delayed it, and D&D spoils turned into homes in one shared small town, Alder Creek, where the band's stewardship helps the townsfolk prosper and every tale ends by planting the town's vote: every person casting a ballot on the township's future and on who should lead it, in nine different wordings of the same conviction — a vote is how you exercise your individuality and control the future state of affairs; don't sit it out; have your voice. The dragons are both kinds: Vargath, slain at Cinder Pass, and Aurelith, won by a treaty earned — the high peaks for her, the low road for the town, sealed with a feast and a signed scroll.

Then the tales were wired into the war. The move a player taps is **readied** for that hero's next battle — one themed bonus, spent when the battle ends: Voting Arrows strikes +2/+2 on Maya's first attack; Scout's Omen lends +1 to all her rolls; Swift Brace blunts the first counter she takes; the Axe of Freedom opens +4 heavy; Freedom's Shout stops The Fade's first counterattack cold, exactly as it froze the troll at Gallows Bridge; Iron Guard cuts the first counter and reflects 2 back; the Sword of Youth hits +2 and restores Hope; Healing Hands mends the band's most-wounded hero as the battle opens; the Ward of Dawn softens her first counter and lifts Hope. The nine scenes were then each given their own animation — arrows streaking, an eye-glint omen, a snapping brace arc, an overhead chop, sound rings pouring from Dre's mouth and no other tile, a steel shield-wall taking a phantom blow, an X of sword-light, a heartbeat glow on healing hands, a dawn dome dissolving a dark bolt — nine different movements for nine different moves, all drawn over the pencil sketches, no new artwork files.

### Built for first-time players

The last passes were about a stranger picking the game up cold. A novice help package added a four-step flow strip to the opening screen ("Read the fact → answer from it → roll to strike him → break him to 40 HP by Battle 16 — or land a Death Blow"), a line explaining why the facts are the weapon, three one-time coach hints, and "?" help dots beside every unexplained moment. When a bug report showed the send-into-battle buttons confusing players, sending became a deliberate two-step: the first tap arms the chosen fighter green — "✓ Selected — tap again to enter battle" — and only the second tap launches. When The Fade's fact-check showed a correction without the claim it corrected, the card was fixed to pair them forever: **"His claim:"** — his exact line — above **"The documented fact:"**, and the same pairing rule was applied to his Sound Bites. When his "Excuse of the hour" looked like a button, it was restyled as what it is — a labeled status chip — and his line gained an unmistakable pill button: FACT-CHECK HIS LINE. And when the nine moves needed explaining where the choice is made, each tile gained its own "?" — the bonus in plain words, plus when to pick it — because one shared help entry for a whole section does not explain individual choices.

### The module, the doors, and the art ruling

The D&D module joined the game in its own repository home that day, as a second page beside the game (module.html) — still Module VF-1, eight encounters, awaiting a catch-up build whose drafts (VF-2, VF-2.1, VF-3) are written and sit with the author for a ruling. The game itself lives at two doors — **vote-or-fade-away.vercel.app**, the address to share, and its GitHub Pages twin — byte-identical at 95 commits by nightfall, the whole game in a single file of about 1.7 MB. The last open question of the day was whether the move scenes should be redrawn as fully new artwork. The author's ruling: no. The pencil sketches and their scenes are the final look — in his words, the game is "fun and light and totally acceptable as is."

### Before dawn: the module rebuilt, and the four endings

The work did not stop at nightfall. First, the module's catch-up: the author approved the three drafts whole — VF-2, VF-2.1, and VF-3 — including the two numbers that had been proposals rather than rulings (the table Death Blow's window and terms; the table Power Card's flat, unscaled 12, with Canvass at camp). The rebuilt module replaced module.html in place, at the same address: sixteen encounters and the Boss Gate, the Death Blow as a DM rule box, The Fade's difficulty dials with their Threat Level, the dice layer, the Logic Engine move library, Hard mode's Fake News Gambits with the correction read aloud either way, both lore boxes, and the Power Card living in the DM's tray as a working five-pip tracker. When the author found the file opened as code from the repository view, the access story was settled plainly — the DM opens the module as a page, at its web address — and, at his direction, the game's opening screen gained a card of its own: "Running the tabletop version?" → *Open the DM Guide — the tabletop module*, one relative link that resolves correctly at both doors.

Then the author caught a hole in the game's own logic: a player could fight all sixteen battles, keep the band standing, and still finish with The Fade above the 40 HP bar — and the game had no ending for that run at all; it silently took the loss screen. The missing ending was imagined in chat before a line of code was written. **Went the Distance**: first light instead of sunrise; the score line naming the near-miss honestly ("The Fade: 47 HP left. The bar was 40 — you were 7 points from breaking him"); the band battered and upright, the rallied friends arriving as people; and The Fade — defiant, almost admiring — stepping back for the first time: *"You can't kill me in a game, children. I live wherever someone shrugs and stays home."* Maya's answer: *"Then we'll see you on November third."* The plan that follows is reframed by the ending's own logic: the last battle isn't in this game — it's Tuesday, November 3, 2026.

Shaping that ending produced the night's sharpest ruling. The author observed that the main difference was the far green country — and then took the thought further: the far green country is Tolkien's image of what lies *through* death (Gandalf, to Pippin) — heaven, essentially — so it does not belong in a victory. It was removed from the distance ending, then from the win itself, and given to the fall: when the band dies, they walk into a far green country under a swift sunrise, and death is not The Fade's victory over them, only over that run. The win's walk home became the thing it should always have been — empowerment: *"The road home was the same road. They were not the same walkers."* They walk it carrying what they know, the card they played, and each other, toward the leaders they chose, every voice counted, and a town and country bending back toward hope.

And then the author defined the finish above the win — the one the whole game had been pointing at. **The Walk Off**: The Fade broken or dead, all three heroes still standing, and **every hero having played their Power Card at least once** along the way. To make that a goal a player can hunt rather than an accident, each play of the shared card is now credited to the hero whose battle it was cast in, and every character sheet carries a plain marker — "Card cast ✓" or "Card not yet cast." The plain win now names what it lacked ("Dre never played his card." — "Priya was carried home."), because the perfect finish is the thesis completed: youth showing up and battling, their changes fulfilled by voting to empower leaders aligned with those desired changes. The Walk Off's closing line: *"The future was never his to keep. It was always theirs to claim — and today, informed and together, they claimed it."* The four endings now stand as one ladder — the fall, the distance, the win, the Walk Off — four distances from the same hope. Testing added one honest footnote: under the current tuning, counterattacks alone cannot mathematically wipe the band (the hardest possible loss still left heroes standing), so the fall — green country and all — waits as the game's mythic edge; whether to retune for it is the author's open call. The credits, meanwhile, gained two-sentence captions under every replay scene — the moment, then its meaning — while the roll stays locked to the closing song, about twelve seconds a scene, thesis card last as ever.

The last thing recorded that night was not a feature. The author named what the game had become: this "get out the vote" game and module are the first arc of a learning environment for his kids' generation — educational game arcs across the "new to them" territories of adult life: buying a first home, building a financial stability blueprint, career ideas, each its own module. The band persists; Alder Creek, the town the Campaigns tales settled, is the continuing world; the spine is one life in order — civic power, then shelter, then stability, then calling. The environment connects the way this generation likes to be engaged — through gamified lenses, facts riding inside the play rather than lectured alongside it. And the reason underneath all of it, in the author's own framing, is hope for the younger generations and their futures. The game was always that. Now the endings say so.

### After dawn: the record completed, and the author's word

The morning's work began with a correction about the credits themselves. The finale's montage had been described — by Kendo — as replaying the player's own battles; the code said otherwise: seventeen stock action scenes, shuffled, with only two scenes a run could earn (the Death Blow and the Power Card). The author chose the honest build: **true replay highlights**. The game now records each run as it is played — every battle's biggest strike with its real damage, a hero's fall, a camp revival, a lie correctly called — and the credits open with that reel, in battle order, the earned featured scenes first, the seventeen stock scenes following as seamless fill so the roll always fills the song. Each generated caption is two sentences: what happened, then what it meant.

The closing song, meanwhile, was given its name in public. It is **"Return of the Prince" — Part III of "The Necromancer," by Rush, from *Caress of Steel* (1975)**, music by Geddy Lee and Alex Lifeson, words by Neil Peart. The credits now open with a song card saying so: one short verse quoted with attribution as tribute, a plain statement that the recording plays through its official YouTube embed and belongs wholly to its makers, and a link out to the full lyric. The game claims nothing of the song but gratitude.

Then the author drew the boundary the rest of the morning obeyed. The game had drifted, over its long night of building, toward his other mission — roofs, first homes, keys in doors. He ruled it back: **this game is purely get out the vote** — win a battle today to help shape a better future for the three travelers, their peers, and their generation. A purity pass followed: the march is now *the future is the quest*; the heroes' perks speak of voices counted and leaders chosen; the win and the Walk Off trade keys and doors for empowerment and the road home. The Campaigns tales were re-anchored the same way — their spoils no longer buy houses in Alder Creek; they endow its charter, recast its bell, and settle the band as citizens and stewards of a town whose future is decided by its vote. What stayed, deliberately, were the facts: mortgages and homebuying appear in the brief and the battles as documented economics, because that is the record — never as a promise the game makes.

Small honesties accumulated. The game learned to define its own word: a landed Death Blow now names its **overkill** — *more than it took* — and grades the kill by the d6 that made it, from **a narrow defeat** at 1 to **a decisive blow** at 6, the style recorded in the log, the credits, and the module's epilogue; on Hard, a roll of 1–2 is still no kill at all — he hangs on at 1 HP, as ruled. The Power Card grew teeth the author specified: played in a battle, it now **stops The Fade's Mission Creep repair for that battle entirely**, on top of its flat 12 and the two battles without his Spin — and his character sheet explains the arithmetic in the open (Standard 6 → +3 · Hard 9 → +5 · dial at 12 → +6) beside the thesis, stated where a player starts: **charge your Power Card, then play it — don't sit on it.** When the author spotted The Fade at 100 HP in the game and 185 at the table, the mismatch was ruled no mismatch at all: different dice economies, each tuned to its room — and the module now says so under his stat block: *"The video game runs him at 100; different dice, same Fade."* An optional **opening scene** — Alder Creek, the machinery, the three who refused — now sits behind a quiet button on the first screen for players who want the story, and at the head of the DM guide as a boxed read-aloud for tables that do. The two rooms were joined both ways (the module gained its *Back to the Game* pill), the iPhone's status bar was cleared off both pages' tops, and the game's badge was corrected to the honest span: **16 battles · ~25–40 minutes**.

Then the author asked the question this file exists to answer. Did the DM guide carry the battles' questions, The Fade's tricks with their corrections, the fact-checked reasoning, and — for each lesson — the part of the overnight exchange that inspired it, with its anchored sources? The audit said: partly, and mostly no. The module had encounters, mechanics, and one-line lore, but not the lineage. So the guide gained a **Sources & Story** appendix: for every encounter, the source stated in full — EIA chokepoints, the IAEA's March statement, the Treasury's 5.31% close, NBER incidence, Treasury and CBO for the inheritance — beside one line naming the insight from that night that made it a lesson: the warning record, the manufactured-crisis analysis, the anatomy of a claim, the flipping-sides file. The traps are printed whole — all **eight** of them, his claim beside the documented fact beside its source — the way the game teaches them. And the author named what this markdown is, in his own judgment: potentially important to a bigger buildout — an educational-gamification venture — because the record is the curriculum's **provenance spine**: the auditable chain from real research, to lesson, to game, that any future arc (shelter, stability, calling) will be built on.

The morning's last build was framing, and it came from the author talking about why. His own stance he states plainly — he is, in his words, a vote-blue camper — and in the same breath he anchored the game away from it: the game exists so youth reach **their own** resolute stances, through living life positively and through helping hands in service of friends — the foundational pillars, he said, of an independent free thinker's logic, reasoning, and will to act. The audit found those pillars already in the machinery — the card that charges only on knowing, the camp that exists to carry friends, the encounter that teaches *vote the record, across party lines if that's where it leads* — and quietest exactly at the mile-high moments: the first screen and the last words. Three small touches closed the gap, his locked thesis line untouched: an opening line under the thesis panel — *"No one here will tell you how to vote — that's the point. Check the claim. Weigh the record. A stance you reasoned out yourself is the one you'll still be standing by in ten years."* — and an **Author's word**, standing last in the credits before the thesis card and read aloud at the table's epilogue: that the game argues for no party and no candidate; that it argues for thinking for yourself, living with open hands, carrying each other — and it closes on the sentence the author gave it that morning: *"And know this: your future can be — and should be — a bright and welcoming place to travel. Go meet it together."*

## The Road to Election Day — the Expansion Ships (October 26 – November 1, 2026)

The week after the first edition went live, the game grew into its full shape — and then, piece by piece, everything in it was made honest.

The expansion arrived first: a band the player chooses (three, four, or six heroes, with one Audible in reserve), sixteen battles, The Bait, five minions who interrupt the fight carrying misinformation of their own — the Disinfo Imp, the Tariff Wraith, the Rate-Freeze Golem, the Spiral Specter, and the Golden Parachute, the CEO who gets paid either way — and, at the front of every run, the opener step: the first hero into Battle 1 is chosen before the fight, and The Fade, who always sees his next opponent, aims his first jab at that hero by name.

Maya's portraits became real choices: four hair colors across four skin tones on her Trail look, wired in and fit-approved, with a one-tap road back to her default look. The game gained an opening worth the name — "Prologue — The Quiet," read aloud at the table and in the game, with Pin's voice one tap away — and the module gained its DM Guide, its secret Fall protocol, and The Brighten: the ending that refuses to be an ending, in which a wiped-out band is revived by an ethereal presence, health restored by the DM's own d20s, every Power Card charged full, and the players sent home with the thesis instead of a defeat. The Fall itself was ruled mythic in the game — a legend nobody reaches — and at the table it lives only in the DM's hands, behind a button that stays dark until every hero is at zero, and opens onto The Brighten when it finally lights.

Then the Flip. The game's Hope meter — a score that drained when you stumbled — was retired completely, in the game and at the table, because a meter that punishes speaking up argues against the game's own thesis. In its place: the Voice Power Meter. It starts at zero and only grows. Wrong answers teach and cost nothing; your voice rises when the band speaks with facts, plays its cards, banishes lies, and knocks on doors. Three endings now read the final tally — A Voice, Heard; The Town Answers; Alder Creek Decides — every one of them positive, because a low tally is a beginning, not a failure. A jackpot joined the Canvass: roll a natural 8 on the street and your own Power Card gains a bonus pip, because sometimes the whole street comes out.

The words were made evergreen. Every "midterm" became "election night," every hard-coded November 3 dissolved into the same generic frame — the game was built for one election and rebuilt to serve them all. The dice were rebuilt too: what had been abstract shapes became the actual polyhedra — a tetrahedron d4 for healing, blessings, and omens, a pip d6 for the Death Blow, an octahedron d8 for the Canvass, an icosahedron d20 for the fight — and the record was corrected where an older draft still named a three-sided die the game no longer uses. Eli Tran's weapon became The Keeper's Bell everywhere it appears, portraits included — the keys retired on theme-purity grounds, a bell in his hands in all three lights — because a game about gathering people should arm its keeper with the thing that calls them together.

Last, the two openings became one. The Age of Reckoning scene retired; its two best beats — what The Fade is, and the charge to play your card — folded into The Quiet, and Pin re-recorded the merged text so the voice and the page agree word for word. The title screen now reads: "Sixteen battles. One boss. A card in every hand."

On the night of November 1, 2026 — two days before Election Day — the expanded edition was published to the game's doors, replacing the first edition in the same home-screen icons families already had. Play the game. Make your move count. Play your power card in hand — cast your vote.
